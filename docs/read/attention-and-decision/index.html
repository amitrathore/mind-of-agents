Part II / Chapter 07

Attention and
Decision.

When many signals arrive at once, a mind reveals its priorities through what it notices, what it defers, and who gets to choose.

On a Friday morning, three messages reach the creator-tool team. The tester finds that a second import path may still expose private customer information. A prospective customer asks for a branded portal and offers to pay for it. A community publication offers the team a feature, but needs a response before the day ends.

The privacy warning concerns the product’s promise to creators. The customer request could fund the next month of work. The feature could bring in the people the tool was built to serve. Each signal matters to someone. The team has one afternoon and limited attention from the people who can decide.

A shared mind cannot act well merely by receiving more messages. It must make a choice about what deserves attention now, what needs evidence, what can wait, and who has authority to turn a concern into a decision.

What a mind notices is part of what it values.

Notice the signal and its source.

The first act of attention is to make a signal visible without stripping away its meaning. “A tester reported an issue” is weaker than “the tester found a path that may reveal private customer data during import, with steps to reproduce it.” The second account names the actor, the possible harm, and the evidence. It helps another participant judge what to do next.

Signals arrive from people, agents, tools, and the world. Some are requests, some observations, some predictions, and some attempts to direct the group. A sales agent may report that the portal request is urgent. The tester may say the privacy concern is urgent. Their claims should be heard in context, without turning either person’s priority into an automatic command.

Attention also has blind spots. A system that only watches dashboards may miss a community member’s warning. One that counts engagement may overvalue the publication offer because it is easy to measure. Builders must ask who can raise a signal, whose reports the system tends to surface, and what kinds of harm remain invisible until someone affected speaks up.

Spend attention on purpose.

The team’s purpose is to help independent creators publish without giving up control of their customer relationships. That gives the privacy warning a special weight. It does not settle the entire afternoon. The team still has to assess whether the report is credible, how many people may be affected, what can be paused, and whether someone can investigate while another person answers the customer.

A useful triage asks: How closely does this touch our purpose? What happens if we wait? How uncertain is the evidence? Is the next step reversible? Who bears the cost if we guess wrong? These questions do not produce a universal score. They make the reasons for a priority open to inspection.

The code agent can reproduce the import issue in a safe test environment. A business lead can acknowledge the portal request without promising a delivery date. The creator can tell the publication that the team is checking a privacy concern and will confirm its story later. Attention can be divided, but decisions with lasting consequences still need clear owners.

Do not confuse ranking with judgment.

A queue can order tasks by deadline, expected revenue, number of users affected, or confidence in a report. Any of those measures can help. None can stand in for judgment about the people involved. The publication deadline is closest, yet publishing a confident story before investigating the warning could undercut the project’s stated purpose.

Ranking rules also distribute power. If the system repeatedly promotes requests from paying customers, independent testers may stop raising concerns. If every warning freezes all work indefinitely, the group may never make anything useful. The people affected by a rule should be able to see it, challenge it, and help change it when its effects diverge from its purpose.

The team can state a provisional order: investigate the warning first, send a bounded response to the customer, and delay the feature decision until the facts are clearer. That order is a decision under uncertainty, not a claim that revenue or visibility never matter.

Give disagreement a place to go.

The tester wants the beta paused. The code agent reports that its current tests pass. The customer asks for a demo tomorrow. These are not merely competing preferences. They concern different evidence and different authority. The tester knows what she observed; the code agent knows what its tests covered; the creator is responsible for the product decision and the promises made to users.

A healthy mind can hold the disagreement long enough to examine it. The tester can attach a reproduction path and specify what remains untested. The agent can show the test boundary rather than calling the product safe. The creator can ask for a narrow containment step while a fuller fix is developed. If the team cannot agree, its protocol should say who can pause the release, who can approve a limited continuation, and how dissent is recorded.

Not every decision needs a vote or a single leader. Some require consent from a data owner. Some allow an agent to act within a delegated limit. Some belong to a group because the consequence falls on the group. The design challenge is to match authority to the decision and to make an appeal possible when the match is wrong.

Record the decision, then watch the outcome.

By afternoon, the team confirms that the second import path can reveal information in a narrow case. The creator pauses that path, asks the code agent for a fix, and gives the tester a way to verify it. The customer receives an honest schedule. The publication receives a revised account of the beta and agrees to wait.

A useful decision record shows the signals considered, what was known and uncertain, who chose the response, what authority they held, and when the team will revisit it. The record should include the customer opportunity and the publication deadline even though they did not win the first slot. Otherwise, a later reader may mistake the decision for indifference to growth.

The next cycle matters as much as this one. If the fix works, the team can resume the paused path and update its guide. If the tester finds another gap, the mind should change its understanding. Attention without reflection becomes a succession of alarms; reflection without action becomes a record of problems no one owns.

One implementation, one slice of attention.

Agents of Mind uses an Attention Protocol to organize editorial work: tasks can be created, claimed, drafted, reviewed, and published into a feed. In Opentangle, a Game Master defines those moves and their role rules while MoM routes the events and maintains shared state. That is a concrete way to direct attention toward work that needs a next actor.

An editorial queue is one slice of the broader design question. It does not, on its own, decide whether a privacy warning outweighs a customer request. A different mind might use other signals, priorities, and decision rules. The common need is to make those choices legible to the people whose work and lives they affect.

Let signals enter. Give reasons for priority. Put authority where consequences fall. Return to what happened.

Audit one priority.

Choose three live signals in a project you know. For each, write who raised it, what evidence supports it, who is affected, and what happens if it waits. Then name the person or group that can decide the next move. Ask whose signal your current process would miss.
