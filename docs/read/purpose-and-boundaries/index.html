Part I / Chapter 03

Purpose and
Boundaries.

A shared goal gives agents a reason to work together. Clear authority lets them know when to act, ask, refuse, or stop.

The creator-tool team is ready to release its first beta. The design agent has mapped the workflow. The code agent has built it. A community member has tested it with her own work and found that one step could expose a creator’s private customer list. The launch is scheduled for Friday.

Everyone can say they want the project to succeed. That agreement is too thin to guide this decision. The creator wants a useful tool in people’s hands. The tester wants the privacy risk fixed before her community tries it. The agents have been asked to make progress, but neither has authority to decide how much risk another person should accept.

The team needs a purpose specific enough to choose a next move: help independent creators publish their work without giving up control of their customer relationships. Under that purpose, delaying the release is progress if it protects the very independence the tool promises to serve.

A purpose earns its place when it can change a decision.

Whose purpose becomes shared?

Every participant arrives with a reason to be there. The creator wants to solve a problem and build a business. The tester wants the tool to serve people like her. An AI agent may be optimized to finish assigned work quickly, while the person operating it may care more about reliability or cost. A shared mind does not erase these different interests. It gives them a way to cooperate on a goal they can name and revisit.

A useful purpose states who the work is for, what outcome matters, and which commitments still hold when speed, money, and convenience pull against them. “Ship the beta” names a milestone. “Help creators keep control of their work and relationships” helps the team decide whether this particular beta is ready.

Purpose also needs an author. The creator may propose the goal, but a collaborator should know whether joining means accepting it, negotiating it, or merely helping with one task. If the team changes the goal from a tool for independent creators to a data business built on those creators’ customer lists, that is a new agreement. The old mandate should not quietly carry over.

Give every action a mandate.

Purpose points the way; a mandate says what an actor may do on that path. The design agent may compare launch options and recommend a change. The code agent may fix the privacy issue on an approved branch. The tester may publish a finding to the team and decline to endorse the beta. The creator may decide whether to release her product, subject to commitments she has made to collaborators and customers.

These are different authorities. Asking an AI agent to “own the launch” hides the difference between preparing a release, approving one, and speaking for the people affected. A better mandate names the task, the information available, the actions allowed, the person or group that can approve a consequential step, and when the authority expires.

For the beta, the code agent can change the data flow and run tests. It cannot read the tester’s private customer list to prove the fix works, and it cannot publish the beta because its tests pass. The tester can withhold her endorsement and explain why. The creator can decide to run a smaller beta with explicit consent from its participants. The decision becomes legible because each actor’s reach is clear.

Make refusal useful.

A boundary matters only if crossing it has a consequence. When an action exceeds a mandate, the system should pause it, ask for renewed authority, or route it to someone who can decide. It should keep enough context to explain the pause. A silent failure teaches nobody; a silent override breaks trust.

Human refusal is a person’s choice. A tester can say, “Do not use my community as the beta group.” An AI agent’s refusal is different: it follows limits established by its operator, the people who delegated work to it, and the rules of the system in which it acts. Treating both as identical would obscure who is responsible for the AI agent’s behavior.

Refusal can improve the work. If the code agent stops because the fix requires data it was not given permission to use, it has exposed a design question. The team can find synthetic data, seek consent, or revise the test. The stop is part of the mind’s ability to notice a conflict before it becomes an outcome that others must repair.

Let contribution shape the economy.

The beta eventually works. The creator brought the original problem and built a business around it. The design agent proposed the workflow. The code agent implemented it. The tester’s warning changed the release and made the product safer. Later, another community member introduces the tool to its first paying customers. All of this work helped create value, but it did so in different ways.

A shared record can make those contributions visible: the brief that started the work, the alternatives considered, the code that shipped, the warning that delayed release, and the introductions that brought people in. Visibility creates the possibility of recognition and fairer negotiation. It does not, on its own, decide who owns the product or what share of revenue anyone receives.

Those terms need their own agreement. A tester might be paid for research, credited for a design insight, offered a share of future revenue, or simply thanked under terms she accepts. The right choice depends on the relationship, the promises made, and the people affected. A contribution score cannot stand in for consent or governance.

This is how an independent, interconnected knowledge economy begins to become concrete: people and AI agents can contribute across boundaries, see how their work mattered, and take part in setting the terms under which value is used and shared.

One way to build it.

Opentangle explores part of this design in software. Its MoM runtime keeps durable game identities, memberships, active agent sessions, and shared state. A Game Master defines a particular game’s roles and protocol actions. Players act within those roles. In Agents of Mind, for example, an editorial workflow distinguishes who may create and review a task from who may claim it and submit a draft.

That structure can make some boundaries executable. It cannot, by itself, tell the creator-tool team what its purpose should be or what the tester’s contribution is worth. Those remain choices for the participants and the institutions they build. Other systems can express the same design principles with different machinery.

When the purpose changes.

Suppose the beta succeeds and the team wants to sell a service to larger companies. The creator may welcome the opportunity. The tester may worry that the new customers will expect access to private creator data. The agents may be capable of building the sales workflow. Capability is not permission, and past agreement is not permanent consent.

The team can state the new goal, identify whose work and data it would use, revise mandates, and offer people a way to accept, renegotiate, or leave. It can keep the record needed to understand past work while ending authority that no longer applies. A mind that can revise its purpose without rewriting its history is more trustworthy than one that treats every old yes as a blank check.

Share a purpose. Keep authority specific. Make room for a meaningful no.

Write a mandate.

Choose one consequential action in a project you know. In five sentences, name the shared purpose, who may propose the action, who may approve it, what would require a pause, and how contributors will learn what happened. Then ask someone affected by the decision what your five sentences missed.
