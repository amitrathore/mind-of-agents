Part III / Chapter 10

Trust,
Contribution,
and Value.

When independent minds create something together, the record of what happened should help them trust, recognize, and challenge the terms of the result.

The creator tool reaches its first public release. A community of independent creators helped shape it. A tester caught a privacy flaw before launch. A publication explained why the work mattered. A distribution partner brought people to the new product. A few of those people become paying customers.

The outcome belongs to no single story. The product team built and maintained the tool. The community made it relevant. The tester prevented a mistake whose cost would have been easy to overlook. The publication took a risk on its readers’ trust. The partner introduced customers who might not otherwise have arrived. Now that money, attention, and opportunity are moving, each participant wants to know how their part will be seen.

A record can show what happened. People still have to decide what it means.

Trust a claim for a reason.

The team can trust the tester’s report about the import path because it can examine her reproduction steps and the change they prompted. It may trust the publication’s account of its interview while still checking a claim about the product’s release date. It can trust a partner to make introductions without giving that partner access to creator contact lists. Trust is specific to a relationship and a kind of action; it need not become an all purpose score.

For a consequential claim, ask who made it, what they could know, which evidence supports it, and whose mandate allowed them to speak. A testimonial is different from a verified test result. A referral event is different from proof that one person caused a purchase. A published byline is different from a complete account of who researched, edited, or approved a piece.

Trust also needs a way to change. A creator may correct a quotation. The tester may discover that the fix covers one path but misses another. A buyer may report that a promised outcome did not arrive. A system that only adds positive signals will grow more confident even as its evidence gets worse. A useful mind can preserve the original claim and show why it was revised.

Make contribution visible without flattening it.

Suppose the first sale follows a link shared by the distribution partner. That link is useful evidence of one route by which the buyer arrived. It does not tell us whether the community’s feedback made the product worth buying, whether the tester’s intervention made it safe enough to recommend, or whether the publication’s explanation gave the buyer confidence. These contributions did different work at different times.

A contribution record can name an actor, an action, an artifact or decision it touched, and the outcome it helped make possible. It should carry provenance: the source of a draft, the review that changed it, the warning that delayed a release, or the introduction that brought in a customer. Where possible, keep the evidence close enough for participants to inspect. Where the evidence is private, show the claim and its limits without exposing someone else’s data.

Visibility must leave room for work that does not produce a neat event. The person who maintained a relationship, translated between groups, or stopped a harmful release may have changed the outcome more than the person who made the last click. An attribution rule can answer a narrow operational question, such as which referral qualifies for an agreed commission. It should not pretend to be a universal measure of merit.

Authorship, contribution, ownership, and reward are separate questions. A writer may own her original draft while allowing a publication to use an edited version. A tester can be credited for finding a flaw without publishing the private report. A distributor can earn an agreed fee without claiming to have designed the product. Keeping these relationships distinct makes recognition more honest.

Agree on value before it is captured.

Money matters, but it is only one form of value. The creator may want payment and control of her customer relationships. The tester may want the finding fixed, a truthful record of her work, and the option to share what she learned. The publication may want a reliable story and a clear right to correct it. The distribution partner may want a commission under terms it can understand before recommending the product.

The groups can make those terms explicit while the work is still open. What is being contributed? How may it be used? Which evidence will count for credit or payment? When is a result final enough to trigger an agreed share? Who absorbs a refund, a correction, or a failed promise? What can a participant take with them if they leave? These questions are easier to negotiate before a successful outcome makes every interpretation valuable.

An agreement cannot foresee every case. It can still name a way to revisit a claim. If the partner’s referral is recorded but the buyer says the community’s guide was decisive, the agreed commission rule may still apply. The broader account of contribution can include both. If the rule itself proves harmful, participants need a way to change it for future work rather than secretly rewriting past terms.

Let a challenge reach the record.

The publication releases its story and credits the product team and the distribution partner. The tester sees no mention of the warning that changed the launch. She does not need a public score to contest this. She needs a place to point to the report, explain the omission, and ask for a correction that respects the privacy of the people involved.

The publication can inspect the evidence, ask the team what it can verify, and amend its account. The record should show both the original claim and the correction. A disputed payment may require a different path from a disputed byline. An accountable design names who can pause a consequential action, who can hear a challenge, and how a decision becomes visible to the affected parties.

These routes should be proportionate. A typo may need a quick correction. A claim that changes payment or access may need review by someone who did not make the original decision. No interface can make a power imbalance disappear; it can at least stop that imbalance from hiding behind a supposedly objective ledger.

One implementation, several limits.

Opentangle provides concrete examples of how these ideas can be made inspectable. The Attention Protocol carries tasks, review decisions, publication, and bylines. A byline helps readers see who is named, though it is not independent proof of every contribution to a piece. The Market Protocol records offers, accepted terms, and orders. The Coseller Protocol records referral touches and applies a game’s attribution policy to an eligible commission pool. MoM persists the shared state in which these protocols operate.

That is a useful slice of the design, not a general solution to fairness. A referral chain measures a defined kind of influence under defined rules; it does not measure the tester’s prevention of harm or the community’s years of relationship building. The current commerce design has return and payment processor paths, while a general buyer and seller dispute process remains a separate question. A held commission reduces some timing risk, but an already settled payout is not automatically reversed by a later return. Those edges show why records, rules, and avenues for challenge must be considered together.

Other systems may use different records and different payment rails. The transferable design is simpler: make a claim traceable, state what it is allowed to prove, agree on the consequences, and leave room for a participant to correct the account.

See the work. Name the terms. Let people challenge the story told about their contribution.

Trace one value chain.

Choose a result that two or more people or agents helped create. List the contributions that shaped it, including work that prevented harm. For each claim, note the evidence, what may be shared, and what remains uncertain. Then name the agreed forms of value, who can question an attribution, and who may change the rules for the next round.

Chapter 10 / Reading notes

Core idea: trust rests on inspectable, revisable claims; contribution records support recognition but cannot decide fairness alone.
